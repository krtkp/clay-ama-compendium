The biggest mistake people make when vibe coding

Eric N.&Eric E.Sep 25, 20266

Eric N. asked:

What is the biggest mistake people are making when they vibe code and how would he fix them?

Eric Engoron replied:

The biggest mistake people make when vibecoding is assuming that AI knows the proper way to build things without validation and that AI thinks of all of the edge cases, so that you do not have to. The unfortunate truth is that AI only thinks about the context that you give it. So, if you don’t bring up things like security, the fact that you’re building something multi-user, the fact that you’ll eventually have X number of users using the platform, you fall victim to AI making errors in implementation because it’s misinformed.

The ultimate fix for this from my perspective would be that we identify a system prompt that can make AI think about all of these things and ask you a bunch of questions to get to the answers like security etc that you may not have thought of, then we pair that with a set of “vibe-coded approved components”, built by engineers to help vibe coders follow best practices (i.e. the right performant way to paginate a list).

In the short term, while that silver bullet doesn’t really exist, or is at least sort of the way there in a bunch of different places, use plan mode often, and in the early days of building your systems, consult engineers you know to make sure AI is going down the right path and then build upon them.

The gap between vibe coding and enterprise-grade production code

Kartik&EricSep 25, 20265

Kartik P. asked:

How big is the gap now between vibe coding and enterprise grade production code - how do you bring vibe coding up to that standard and enterprise coding to vibe coding simplicity?

Eric Engoron replied:

I think the gap between enterprise grade production code is certainly lessening as AI becomes more advanced, but it’s still very prevalent. As mentioned in previous threads, AI is only as good as the context it is given. So, a lot of the pain points come from AI not having enough information about who you are as a business, how things should be run, how you think about scaling to thousands and thousands of customers etc. There’s also a big difference for writing a salesforce integration that has support for 100000 records, and one that supports over a billion records reliably.

Enterprise to me also can mean you scaling your own company, and then more problems arise. For instance, if I’m charging for AI usage, and eventually need to get audited, did AI build my billing system in a way where I can pass a financial audit, and it will all flow into my ERP system?

In my mind, the best ways to bring it up to standard are:

• Always ask the question about scale
• Hopefully, as i alluded to in a previous thread, we have some type of enterprise primitive that any vibecoding tool can build on top of
• Use conversations with others as the best way to gain context, and make sure your AI is aware of those conversations

Vibe-coding is just like any other skill, you need to learn what you don’t know from others, because known unknowns are easier to think about than unknown unknowns

The future of GTM engineering, and keeping up while things move fast

Eric Engoron replied:

To me, the future of GTM Engineering is two fold:

• 1. Making sure that all of your systems are working together effectively
• 2. Making sure that you’re taking feedback from users of those systems, and making improvements and iterations to those systems.

By working together effectively, what I really mean is that AI will build a bunch of separate systems that achieve similar things, if it doesn’t have full context on what’s already been built. Splitting work between yourself and AI; planning before building

Max&EricSep 25, 20264

Max R. asked:

How do you delegate the work between yourself and AI? What’s your planning process like before you build?

Eric Engoron replied:

My planning process is gaining as much business context as humanly possible. Then, translating that into a spec that includes context on what’s already been built. Then I ask AI to present a plan for how it would build that spec, and I fact check each part of the plan and I ask more questions about why it made certain decisions. Then, I ask it to update the plan with my feedback and only after my final review does it start building.

Because I work mainly on billing and enterprise, my delegation process is all about context building. AI doesn’t have great context on scale or proper billing processes, so the planning process becomes far more important

How non-technical people can become more technical with Clay and LLMs

Andrea B. asked:

What advice would Eric have for non-technical folks trying to become more technical through tools like Clay and LLMs? Vibe-coding advice?

Eric Engoron replied:

The biggest advice that I could possibly give anyone trying to become more technical is that creativity is your greatest strength. It’s funny, because traditionally non-technical roles (i.e. marketing, brand, etc) are traditionally seen as the creative ones, but in my experience, my creativity is how I’ve been able to really build my own engineering knowledge.

Flexible and powerful vs. simple: how integrations and waterfalls are designed

Nishika&EricSep 25, 20262

Nishika G. asked:

Hi Eric! You've built more than half of Clay's 200+ integrations, so I'm curious about the engineering judgment behind that.

When you're deciding how to build a new integration or add it into a waterfall, how do you decide the tradeoff between making it flexible and powerful versus keeping it simple enough that someone new to Clay can actually use it without getting overwhelmed?

Eric Engoron replied:

This is a great question! The answer has actually changed over time.

In the beginning, we would actually take each integration provider, and tailor the responses to work better in Clay to match with other providers, so it would be easier to use. So, we’d rename fields, remove the fields that were confusing (i.e. a revenue amount without a currency or cents vs dollars), and then make sure everything was cohesive.

However, as we started scaling the number of providers, this was not scalable because the providers themselves would be asked questions about the data and then say: “we don’t provide that field” and it would be a whole mess. So, we made the choice to stay true to the provider to allow things to be more flexible, even if the overhead was a bit more.

In terms of using Clay without getting overwhelmed, as we’re building a waterfall, we try to do our best to rank providers to give you the best results for what you need, and that’s going to get better over time as well. Moreover, Sculptor and investing in AI will help bridge the gap between our highly technical power users, and those just coming to Clay with a use-case. The goal is that we’d use our institutional knowledge to help you build effectively.

The skill Eric wishes he'd learned earlier building integrations

Nishika&EricSep 25, 20262

The biggest skill I wish I learned earlier on was how to better convey my technical thoughts to external providers. As you’re building these integrations, you’re working with a variety of different engineers. Each engineer has their own style and their own way of building things. Knowing which problems to solve early on, and how that changed with growth

Kartik&EricSep 25, 20262

Kartik P. asked:

How did you know which problems were the most valuable to solve when you started off, and how did that change as Clay began to grow?

Eric Engoron replied:

This is a fun question! My first job at Clay was actually getting on calls with Varun, and building whatever came out of those sales calls.

We would do this thing called the “reverse demo” where, instead of us demoing our product, we’d give our product to the prospect and then say, “What’s missing”, and literally just watch them go through it on a Zoom call. Then we would do a quick retro on what we saw, and that’s what we would build that day. We wanted to make sure we’d build something that folks would actually use, and the best way to do that was to ask them, in the world of integrations and GTM, what are you missing?

As Clay started to grow, we never lost that customer centric vision, but there was certainly more process around it, and planning cycles that meant I wasn’t just building 6 integrations in a single day anymore. Scale mattered, and so did making sure that these features worked for thousands of customers and not just one

What separates deep technical GTM careers from surface-level integration work

Nishika&EricSep 25, 20261

Nishika G. asked:

Also, If someone wants to build a real technical career in this GTM engineering and automation space, what would you say actually separates the people who grow into deep, valuable technical roles from the ones who stay stuck doing surface-level integration work?

Eric Engoron replied:

Another great question here! To start, I think I’d reframe the notion of surface-level integration work. Sure, certain things in the world of integrations might be simple, like setting up a webhook when a form is submitted to then go to a slack channel. However, at the same time, those surface-level things are also super complex if you look a little deeper. For instance:

• This automation works when we have 50 people a week, but what if I had 5000.
• If I run a commercial and get a bunch of traffic to my site, will this automation break?

So, there’s definitely a lot of deep technical thought to be done in the world of integrations.

With that being said, the biggest separation factor for me is being able to understand the context of why you’re building what you are, and being able to tackle that problem creatively. Often times, you’re given an automation job where someone tells you exactly what they need, but their solution may only be one option. So, what I like to do is take a step back from the solution and say: “What problem does this form automation actually solve”. Then you also start involving things like Systems Architecture into your skillset

Eric Engoron replied:

The biggest thing I’ve learned as my literally job has become sharing context with all of Clay is that nobody wants to read docs anymore. So, the biggest thing that I can suggest is:

• Make sure that decisions between coding sessions are logged in a wiki (it can be ai written) and make sure that future sessions are pointing to that wiki
• For context between team members, documentation becomes more important for historical decision making purposes. How to edit your writing, and how Mishti uses AI to edit

Max&MishtiSep 10, 20266

Max R. asked:

Editing tips! ✏️ It's one thing to write and not have a blank page anymore, but editing is the next hard skill to master. Where's the best place for me to start learning how to edit writing well? What are your personal tips for editing? Do you use AI to help with the editing? If so, how do you use it to help enable you to edit better? If not, where would you use AI in the writing process, if you use it at all?

Mishti Sharma replied:

This is a really great question! I do have a really particular way I use AI to edit and I find it insanely useful. Here are some of my top tips:

• An essay should have clear stakes and a thesis up front.
• Paragraphs in an essay need to be ordered in a way that makes sense.
• Every single paragraph needs a topic sentence that previews what the rest of the paragraph is going to be about.
• Every single paragraph needs a transition out of the last paragraph and into the next paragraph.
• (A lot of the above are about guiding the reader so their mind can follow where you're going)!
• On a micro level cut fluff, cut jargon, cut wordiness, don't use unnecessary adverbs and adjectives, avoid passive voice, be as succinct as possible

The number one thing I do when I'm using AI to edit is ask it to give me 5 versions of an intro hook, or a transition sentence out of one paragraph and into another, or a topic sentence for a paragraph. Then I'll pick what I like the best. I also very frequently paste a paragraph into AI and say, "This feels clunky. Make it flow better" or "There's too much jargon in this. Make it more plainspoken and succinct." It's actually really good at helping I think!

We all know that owners/founders wanna increase revenue and cut costs - but the mechanisms for that in each industry would be different.

For a business owner in the medical field, that might look like being on more insurance providers' panels so they can take on more patients/clients.

For an owner/founder in the ecom space, more efficient ad spend allocation would help them both increase rev while reducing ad costs (double whammy), and whatever product you're marketing to them should be framed as an effective means to that end.

Same for when you're targeting the finance guy, the HR lady, and the operations folks.

Study these verticals and industries deeply, understand the work-related pain-points they suffer everyday + the goals/objectives they're trying everyday to achieve, and how to connect both in your copy (messaging angles).

Developing and training that instinct will give you a huge edge over other GTMEs who get confused about building a full GTM strategy when they're given a new client to handle, and end up writing generic copy and building lists with inaccurate targeting, and don't frame the offer in a way that would be attractive to the target persona(s).

How Clay knew coining the GTM Engineer role was the right call

Mishti Sharma replied:

Thanks for the question! We definitely didn't think GTM engineering would necessarily take off. We were just responding to an internal need when we started hiring for the role, before we even wrote about it. Then we noticed that a lot of the companies we were selling to were super intrigued by the work our early GTMEs were doing — and they wanted to hire GTMEs too!

Little organic signals like that that made us feel like we should talk publicly about the role more. (Even then, there was no guarantee that it would really catch on beyond a certain tiny niche of us and our peers.)

I think the key thing here is we never started with "oh, we need to stand out in the market — what roles or brand campaigns will do that"? GTM engineering at Clay happened very organically based on a true internal need + the changes in the market (AI changing how growth was done and what was possible).

I don't think we're going to be coining more titles, but who knows 🙂 The key thing is that coining a title or a brand campaign in isolation really rarely works. It really has to be the perfect combination of how something your company helps with hooks into a current need, change in the market, or topic of discussion I think!
